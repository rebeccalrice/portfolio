Ch. 1

What is it?

It doesn't look dangerous. It looks like an ordinary pill, the kind someone takes for a bad back, or to stay awake through a shift. But it can hold two milligrams of fentanyl, about as much as 10 to 15 grains of table salt. You can't see it, taste it, or smell it. And it can stop someone's breathing.

The uneven mix

These are mixed in blenders or buckets, not sterile labs. The powder never blends evenly. Two people can split the same pill; one feels fine, the other gets a deadly dose.

The filler

Almost the entire pill is filler, binder and dye. That's why you can't judge a pill by how heavy it is, what it smells like, or what it looks like.

Two milligrams

About as much as 10 to 15 grains of salt. You can't see it inside the pill, but it's enough to stop someone's lungs from taking a breath.

The fake stamp

It's pressed to look exactly like a real pain pill from a pharmacy. The metal stamps that make these are sold online, cheaply.

It's mixed by hand, with no one measuring, so the line between relief and death comes down to fractions of a milligram.

That's not a small risk. That's most of them.

Ch. 2

How does it get here?

It never touched soil. Older drugs needed fields of poppies and a harvest. Fentanyl starts in chemical factories.

The raw ingredients are poured into industrial barrels and cross the Pacific on ordinary cargo ships.

It starts as chemicals, not drugs. China is the main source of the chemicals used to make fentanyl, and of the machines that press it into pills.

India is a growing second source.

Many of these chemicals have ordinary, legal uses too, which makes them hard to track.

They cross the Pacific by ship and by plane, often labeled as something else, and arrive at ports on Mexico's west coast.

In Mexico, cartels turn those chemicals into fentanyl. Then they press it into pills made to look exactly like real medicine.

Most of it doesn’t come across open land. It comes through the front door.

It’s hidden inside ordinary cars and trucks, waiting in line at official border crossings, in the same lanes commuters and families use every day.

In 2025, officers found 11,486 pounds of fentanyl at the U.S.–Mexico border.

86 percent of it was caught at official crossings. And 96 percent came through California and Arizona.

That's only what they caught.

Nobody knows how much made it through.

Ch. 3

The 70mph Pipeline

Once it's across, it doesn't sneak. It drives, on the same highways you do.

From the crossings near San Diego, it heads north on I-5 up the coast, and on I-15 through Las Vegas toward Salt Lake City.

From Nogales, Arizona, I-19 runs to Tucson. There it meets I-10, a highway that crosses the whole country, from Los Angeles to Florida.

From El Paso, I-25 heads north to Denver. From Laredo, I-35 runs straight up the middle of the country: Dallas, Oklahoma City, Kansas City, Minneapolis.

Where those highways cross the ones running east and west, it spreads out.

The same roads that bring groceries to your town bring this.

Somewhere along that road, there's an exit like any other, a few miles from a town like yours.

And then it takes an off-ramp. A town of 10,000 people, with a grain elevator, a high school football field, and quiet gravel roads.

Overdose deaths have finally started to fall across the country. But not everywhere, and not for everyone. When an interstate runs past your town, nowhere is truly far away.

It could be any town.

It could be yours.

The streetlights stay on all night.

Behind one of those windows, someone is asleep.

Or seems to be.

Ch. 4

What does it do?

Breathing is something your body does without asking you. Fentanyl turns that switch off.

Within minutes, breathing slows and grows shallow.

The person looks like they've simply drifted off to sleep. They might snore heavily, or make raspy, gurgling sounds.

It isn't violent or loud. That's why it's so easy for someone in the next room to walk by, thinking they're just tired.

Their lips and fingertips turn a faint blue or gray. They won't wake up if you say their name or shake their shoulder.

Without oxygen, the brain can start to suffer damage in about four minutes.

This is where naloxone, often known as Narcan, steps in. It works like a key, knocking fentanyl off the brain's receptors so the body remembers to breathe again.

You don't need a medical degree or a prescription to keep it in your purse or your cabinet.

Save this

If someone won't wake up

  1. 1

    Call 911.

  2. 2

    Spray naloxone into one nostril. Lay them on their side so their airway stays clear.

  3. 3

    Stay with them. Naloxone can wear off before fentanyl does.

  4. 4

    If they don't respond in 2 to 3 minutes, give a second dose in the other nostril.

Naloxone won’t harm someone who hasn’t taken opioids. If you’re not sure, give it.

Ch. 5

Who does it take?

Before the numbers, one more thing to notice.

Since you started reading

You've been reading for a few minutes.

On average, someone in America dies of an overdose every seven and a half minutes.

An average, not a live count. Based on about 70,000 overdose deaths in the U.S. in 2025 (CDC, provisional).

They weren't statistics.

They were somebody's son, somebody's mom, the coworker who covered your shift, the kid you sat next to in homeroom. Most of them weren't trying to die. They were trying to feel okay.

And here's what often gets overlooked: people do come back. One in eleven American adults is living in recovery right now. That's about 23.5 million people, living full lives.

Here's the truth that rarely makes the evening news: we are turning the corner.

In 2025, about 38,000 fewer families lost someone than at the peak in 2022.

Because more everyday people carry naloxone, and more parents and friends know the warning signs, the tide is moving.

Each dot is 1,000 people who died of a drug overdose. CDC final data through 2023; 2024 and 2025 are provisional.

Every one of those dots was a thousand people.

And every one of them lived somewhere.
